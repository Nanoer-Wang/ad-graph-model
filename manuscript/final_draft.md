## Abstract

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is driven by the self-assembly of amyloid-β (Aβ) peptides into toxic oligomers and fibrils, yet most mechanistic models of the amyloid cascade treat aggregation as an abstract source term and do not represent the fibril interface at which aggregation is catalysed. Here we develop a continuous-time, network-resolved model of AD progression in which the fibril interface is an explicit, druggable state. Monomer, toxic-oligomer, fibril, passivator, interface-occupancy and neuronal-damage variables evolve on a brain network; surface-mediated aggregation (secondary nucleation and elongation) is proportional to the free-interface fraction (1 − θ), so that any agent occupying fibril interfaces reduces the aggregation efficiency and, downstream, neurotoxicity. We derive a quasi-steady-state (Langmuir) description of passivator binding and an analytic expression for the critical passivation fraction θc above which the aggregation cascade is arrested; for the reference parameterisation θc = 0.55. Because neuronal damage feeds back on monomer production, the full system arrests only at a substantially higher passivation fraction (θ ≈ 0.93), and the difference between the two thresholds quantifies the feedback penalty that any interface-targeting therapy must overcome. The model exhibits genuine bistability at strong passivation: a healthy low-damage state and a diseased high-damage state coexist over a range of clearance and passivation levels, separated by a saddle, which defines a therapeutic window — early, sufficiently strong passivation prevents the transition to the diseased state, whereas delayed or sub-threshold intervention only slows progression. Because trajectories below the fold approach the diseased state with critical slowing down, finite-horizon assessments can mistake slowing for arrest; separating the two requires continuation of the full system, and the distinction is what makes combination therapy necessary rather than merely beneficial. Latin-hypercube sampling with partial rank correlation coefficients identifies monomer supply (production and clearance) as the dominant control on every outcome, with the secondary-nucleation coefficient, oligomer clearance and the toxicity-to-damage gain as the leading actionable parameters, and shows that network-transmission parameters are not significant at the network level; a synthetic-benchmark identifiability analysis shows that the key kinetic parameters are jointly recoverable from trajectory data with profile-likelihood confidence intervals. Combination-therapy simulations show that clearance enhancement and interface passivation act supra-additively: evaluated on durable (long-horizon) outcomes, the combination arrests the cascade at a reference dose at which neither agent alone has any durable effect — a threshold-crossing synergy that identifies a broad, mappable region of the two-dimensional dose space. The framework links molecular aggregation kinetics, network propagation and intervention design in a single mathematically transparent model, and provides a quantitative basis for evaluating fibril-interface-targeting therapies for AD.

**Keywords:** Alzheimer's disease; amyloid-β; secondary nucleation; fibril interface; passivation; mathematical model; bifurcation analysis; global sensitivity analysis; parameter identifiability

## 1. Introduction

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is the most common neurodegenerative disorder and a leading cause of dementia in ageing populations. Its defining molecular pathology is the assembly of the amyloid-β (Aβ) peptide into soluble oligomers, protofibrils and insoluble fibrils that deposit as senile plaques, accompanied by tau neurofibrillary pathology, synaptic loss and regional neurodegeneration [1–4]. The amyloid cascade hypothesis, in its modern form, posits that the production, aggregation and clearance of Aβ constitute the initiating sequence of a pathogenic chain that ultimately produces neuronal death and cognitive decline [1,2]. More than three decades of work have refined this view: Aβ aggregation is now understood as a nucleation–polymerisation process with a lag phase, an exponential growth phase and a plateau [5–8], in which secondary nucleation on the surface of existing fibrils, rather than homogeneous primary nucleation in solution, dominates the production of new oligomers for many amyloid systems [9,10]. The fibril interface is therefore not an inert endpoint of aggregation but its catalytic engine.

This kinetic insight has therapeutic consequences that are only partially exploited. Current disease-modifying strategies — notably anti-Aβ antibodies such as aducanumab and lecanemab — target deposited and soluble Aβ and have shown plaque clearance with modest clinical benefit [11–13]. An orthogonal strategy is to target the aggregation process itself: small molecules and natural products that bind fibril surfaces, redirect aggregation off the toxic pathway, or cap fibril ends can reduce the rate at which new toxic oligomers are generated [14–17]. The green-tea catechin epigallocatechin gallate, for example, remodels existing fibrils and redirects aggregation into inert, off-pathway oligomers [16,17]. We refer to this class of mechanisms, broadly, as **fibril-interface passivation**: the occupation of fibril interfaces by a molecular species that reduces the efficiency of surface-catalysed aggregation. Passivation is attractive because it acts catalytically at the site of oligomer production, requires only partial interface coverage to slow the cascade, and is mechanistically complementary to clearance-based immunotherapy.

Mathematical modelling has contributed substantially to understanding the amyloid cascade, but existing approaches leave the interface mechanism largely implicit. Early and widely used models describe Aβ dynamics as well-mixed ordinary differential equations (ODEs) with empirically parameterised production, aggregation and clearance terms, which capture biomarker trajectories but not the microscopic mechanism that generates toxic species [18–20]. Network models of AD progression, inspired by the observation that neurodegeneration follows large-scale brain connectivity patterns [21–23], describe the spread of pathology along structural or functional connectomes using network-diffusion or epidemic frameworks [24,25]; these models typically treat the local pathology as a single scalar and do not resolve the aggregation kinetics that generate toxicity. Data-driven temporal models such as Subtype and Stage Inference and event-ordering approaches characterise the sequence of biomarker abnormalities with high fidelity [26,27], but are descriptive rather than mechanistic. A model that couples (i) mechanistically resolved aggregation kinetics, including the fibril interface as an explicit state, (ii) network-resolved spatial propagation, and (iii) mathematically rigorous analysis of thresholds, sensitivity and identifiability, is still missing.

Here we develop such a model and use it to ask three questions. First, how much interface passivation is required to arrest the aggregation cascade, and is there a sharp, predictable threshold? Second, given that AD is a progressive disease with a long preclinical phase [28,29], when during progression is passivation still effective — i.e. what is the therapeutic window? Third, which kinetic parameters dominate the outcome, and can those parameters be recovered from observable trajectory data — a prerequisite for translating the model into patient-specific predictions?

Our model extends a previously described conceptual amyloid-cascade framework [30] in three directions: continuous-time kinetics replace discrete threshold updates; a brain network replaces an abstract grid; and, most importantly, an explicit passivation mechanism with a Langmuir occupancy description replaces phenomenological aggregation terms. The principal results are as follows. (i) We derive an analytic critical passivation fraction θc = 1 − (cY + kf)cZ / {Xs[kskf + ke(cY + kf)]}, where Xs is the healthy monomer steady state, which is the exact threshold at which the aggregation subsystem — with damage feedback suppressed — becomes stable; for the reference parameterisation θc = 0.55. Continuation of the full system shows that damage feedback shifts the arrest threshold upward to θ ≈ 0.93, so that θc acts as a transparent lower bound and design principle rather than the operating point. (ii) The full system is bistable at strong passivation, with a saddle separating a healthy from a diseased attractor; this produces hysteresis and a well-defined therapeutic window. (iii) Global sensitivity analysis (Latin hypercube sampling with partial rank correlation coefficients) identifies monomer supply — production and clearance — as the dominant control on every outcome, with the secondary-nucleation coefficient, oligomer clearance and the toxicity-to-damage gain as the leading actionable parameters, and shows that network-transmission parameters are not significant at the network level. (iv) A synthetic-benchmark identifiability analysis demonstrates joint recovery of six kinetic parameters with tight profile-likelihood intervals. (v) Clearance enhancement and interface passivation combine supra-additively, and the two-dimensional dose landscape identifies a broad region in which near-complete neuroprotection is achieved. Together these results provide a quantitative, mechanistically explicit framework for evaluating interface-targeting therapies and for designing combination regimens.

## 2. Model formulation

### 2.1 Network representation

The brain is represented as a weighted graph G = (V, E, W) with |V| = N nodes (brain regions) and non-negative symmetric weights Wvu. Three network families are used: (i) a two-dimensional cortical sheet (a 20 × 20 grid with distance-decaying weights), which serves as a transparent abstraction of contiguous cortical tissue; (ii) a Watts–Strogatz small-world network with N = 68 nodes, which controls for topology; and (iii) a surrogate structural connectome of 68 cortical regions with Desikan-like labels [31], distance-dependent connectivity, hub reinforcement and hemispheric asymmetry, calibrated to reproduce the degree distribution, clustering and path length of macro-scale connectomes. Network transport is conservative: for any quantity x, the flux along edges is Dx times the difference between the weighted neighbour mean and the local value, i.e. Dx(Lx)v with L the graph Laplacian, so that total burden is preserved in the absence of reactions. All quantities are expressed in dimensionless model units; conversion to physical units requires an explicit calibration of the time and concentration scales (Section 2.4).

### 2.3 Quasi-steady-state passivation and aggregation efficiency

Binding and unbinding of passivator to fibril interfaces are fast compared with the disease timescale (kon, koff ≫ δa). Setting the right-hand side of Eq. (6) to zero gives the Langmuir isotherm

![eq_langmuir](images/eq_langmuir.png)

so that a sustained passivator level P occupies a fraction θ* = P/(KD + P) of interfaces, with KD = koff/kon the dissociation constant. Eq. (8) is used in all steady-state and bifurcation calculations, whereas the full binding kinetics are retained in time-dependent simulations (Fig. 5A). The corresponding quasi-steady-state passivator level for an infusion uP is P* = uP/cP.

### 2.4 Nondimensionalisation and the cascade number

Choosing the healthy monomer steady state Xs = λ0/cX as the concentration scale, the same scale for oligomer and fibril (mass-consistent), and the monomer clearance rate cX as the time scale, the model reduces to a dimensionless system governed by the groups ρ = knXs^{m−1}/cX (primary nucleation), σ = ksXsZs^{q−1}/cX (secondary nucleation), ε = keZs/cX (elongation), γ = cY/cX, φ = kf/cX, ζ = cZ/cX (clearances and conversion), κ = DX/cX, λ = DY/cX (transport), K = KY/Ys and h = hY (toxicity response), and the passivator dissociation constant KD. A useful composite is the **cascade number**

the ratio of oligomer production to oligomer removal at the reference state. R ≫ 1 indicates a runaway regime in which clearance cannot keep pace with production; R < 1 indicates a regime in which clearance dominates and the cascade is stable. For the reference parameterisation (Table 1), R ≈ 4.9, i.e. the untreated system operates deep in the runaway regime, which is why modest passivation produces large effects (Section 4.4).

### 2.5 Linear stability and the critical passivation fraction

The healthy state of the well-mixed node is the fibril-poor equilibrium (X ≈ Xs, Y ≈ 0, Z ≈ 0, a ≈ 0). Its stability is governed by the linearisation of the oligomer–fibril subsystem,

dY/dt = ksXs(1 − θ)Z − (cY + kf)Y,

dZ/dt = kfY + [keXs(1 − θ) − cZ]Z,

whose determinant changes sign when (cY + kf)[cZ − keXs(1 − θ)] = ksXskf(1 − θ).

Solving for the passivation fraction gives the **critical passivation fraction**

![eq_theta_c](images/eq_theta_c.png)

For the reference parameters (Table 1), θc = 0.547: more than half of the fibril interfaces must be occupied to stabilise the fibril-poor state of the aggregation subsystem. Eq. (10) makes the mechanism of the therapy transparent: θc falls when oligomer clearance (cY), fibril clearance (cZ) or the conversion rate kf increase, and rises with the surface-reactivity parameters ks and ke. Clearance enhancement and passivation therefore act on the same inequality from opposite sides — the basis for the synergy quantified in Section 4.5. Two qualifications connect this subsystem result to the full model (Section 4.3). First, Eq. (10) is derived with the damage coordinate frozen at a = 0; in the complete system, in which damage feeds back on monomer production and on toxicity, the low-damage state remains unstable until θ ≈ 0.93, so θc is a transparent lower bound on the passivation required in practice rather than the operating point — the difference is the feedback penalty. Second, the full system can retain a high-damage attractor even where a low-damage state exists (bistability), so the level required to arrest the cascade from the healthy state is lower than the level required to escape an established diseased state; their difference is the hysteresis that defines the therapeutic window.

### 2.6 Intervention modelling

Interventions are represented as parameter schedules: at a specified time, selected parameters are changed to new values and held there for the remainder of the simulation. Passivation therapy sets the infusion rate uP at the intervention time, producing a sustained passivator level P* = uP/cP and, after binding equilibration, an interface occupancy θ*. Clearance enhancement increases cY and cZ; nucleation inhibition reduces kn; propagation inhibition reduces DY and ηN. This schedule representation keeps the model linear in the control and makes dose–response, onset-time and combination analyses directly comparable (Sections 4.4–4.6).

## 3. Numerical methods

### 3.1 Network construction

The cortical sheet is a 20 × 20 lattice with eight-neighbour connectivity and distance-decaying weights, row-normalised so that every node redistributes the same total flux. The small-world network is a Watts–Strogatz ring (N = 68, mean degree 6, rewiring probability 0.12) with geometric weights. The surrogate connectome assigns each of 68 Desikan-like regions a three-dimensional coordinate and connects region pairs with probability and weight decaying with Euclidean distance (exp(−d/d0), d0 = 0.45, truncated at d = 0.60), reinforces hub regions (precuneus, posterior and isthmus cingulate, rostral anterior cingulate, superior frontal, middle temporal, inferior parietal, lateral occipital) by a factor of 2.5, attenuates interhemispheric weights by 0.45, adds log-normal weight noise, and rewires a fraction p = 0.10 of the resulting short geometric edges into random long-range edges (association pathways), which is what produces small-world behaviour. Its macro-scale statistics (mean degree 14.4, density 0.215, mean clustering 0.54, characteristic path length 2.13) fall within the range reported for human structural connectomes [31,32]. Simulations are seeded in the entorhinal cortex, consistent with the earliest cross-sectional and longitudinal neuropathological and imaging observations [33,34]. The sheet simulations are seeded in a central node.

### 3.2 Numerical integration

The ODE system (6N variables) is integrated with an adaptive multi-step solver (LSODA) with relative and absolute tolerances of 10−7 and 10−9 over t ∈ [0, 40] on a uniform output grid of 1201 points; long-horizon simulations (Sections 4.4–4.5, 4.8) extend the same integration to t = 200 on an equally dense output grid. Positivity is enforced by clipping at zero inside the right-hand side, which is inactive in all reported simulations. Steady states of the well-mixed node are computed by multi-start bounded least-squares root finding (80–120 starts per parameter value); stability is assigned from the eigenvalues of the numerical Jacobian. All random procedures use fixed seeds for reproducibility.

### 3.3 Outcome metrics

We report: the peak and peak time of the network-mean oligomer burden (Ypeak, tpeak); the area under the network-mean oligomer and damage trajectories (AUCY, AUCa); the terminal mean damage ⟨a⟩end; the fractions of nodes exceeding damage 0.5 and 0.8; the times at which the mean damage crosses 0.2, 0.5 and 0.8; the propagation-front speed and maximum radius (defined by the damage level 0.5); the terminal mean interface occupancy ⟨θ⟩end; and the mean aggregation efficiency 1 − ⟨θ⟩. Composite benefit scores are defined relative to the untreated baseline as the mean of relative reductions in Ypeak, AUCY, AUCa, ⟨a⟩end and the fraction a > 0.8, plus the relative delay of the ⟨a⟩ = 0.5 crossing, clipped to [0, 1].

### 3.4 Global sensitivity analysis

Because local one-at-a-time sensitivities conflate parameter interactions with curvature, we perform global uncertainty and sensitivity analysis following the standard systems-biology protocol [35]. Twenty parameters (all kinetic, transport, toxicity and binding parameters, with the nucleation order m and the secondary-nucleation order q varied over narrow ranges) are sampled by Latin hypercube sampling (n = 400) over ±3-fold ranges around the reference values; the model is simulated for each sample; and partial rank correlation coefficients (PRCC) between ranked inputs and ranked outputs are computed, controlling for all remaining inputs, with two-sided p-values from the t-distribution with n − 2 − k degrees of freedom. PRCC is reported for five outcomes: Ypeak, AUCY, ⟨a⟩end, t⟨a⟩=0.5 and the fraction a > 0.8. The same sample set provides an uncertainty ensemble (5th–95th percentile bands) for the trajectory forecasts.

### 3.5 Parameter identifiability

To assess whether the model's key kinetic parameters can be constrained by trajectory data, we generate a synthetic benchmark: a "true" parameter set (perturbations of kn, ks, ke, cY, ηY and KY relative to the reference values; Table 4) is simulated, sampled at 21 observation times over [0, 40] for the network-mean oligomer, fibril and damage trajectories, and corrupted with 3% heteroscedastic Gaussian noise. Six parameters (kn, ks, ke, cY, ηY, KY) are then re-estimated by multi-start bounded nonlinear least squares (three starts, 4-fold bounds). To remove the scale disparity between parameters spanning two orders of magnitude (kn ≈ 0.01 versus ks ≈ 2), the estimation is performed in log parameter space and the linearised covariance is mapped back with the delta method. Practical identifiability is summarised by (i) the recovery error of each parameter, (ii) profile-likelihood 95% confidence intervals, obtained by re-optimising all other parameters while each parameter in turn is scanned (in log space) and the SSE is compared with the χ² threshold for one degree of freedom, and (iii) the parameter correlation matrix from the linearised (Gauss–Newton) covariance. Prediction bands are obtained by sampling 12 parameter vectors from the linearised covariance. To keep the computational cost of the multi-start search tractable, parameter estimation and profile scans use relaxed solver tolerances (relative 10−5, absolute 10−7); all reported trajectories, including the fitted trajectory in Fig. 8, are re-simulated at the reference tolerances of Section 3.2.

### 3.6 Steady-state and bifurcation computations

One-parameter sweeps of the passivation fraction θ (equivalently, the sustained passivator level P = θKD/(1 − θ)) are continued through the well-mixed steady-state branches, with the passivator held at its sustained quasi-steady value throughout; saddle-node folds are located by bisection on the sign of the largest real part of the Jacobian along a branch, and the therapeutic boundary in the (cY, θ) plane is mapped by bisection on the existence of a low-damage stable state, cross-checked against the analytic prediction Eq. (10). Bistability is verified by forward integration of the full node system from healthy and diseased initial conditions under a sustained infusion that holds P at its quasi-steady value. A pseudo-potential for the slow damage variable is computed from the quasi-steady-state reduction: with (X, Y, Z, θ) slaved to the damage coordinate a, the damage dynamics reduces to the scalar equation da/dt = f(a), whose roots are the fixed points and whose primitive Φ(a) = −∫f da has wells at the stable states. Because the fast aggregation subsystem is contractive only where production is sufficiently suppressed, the reduction — and hence Φ(a) — is defined only on intervals of a that admit a positive equilibrium; the intervening runaway gaps are reported explicitly rather than bridged.

## 4. Results

### 4.1 Baseline disease dynamics

The untreated model reproduces the qualitative phenomenology of sporadic AD: a long silent phase, an amplification phase, and a rapid transition to widespread damage (Fig. 2). The seed region (entorhinal-like) shows the characteristic sequence of monomer accumulation, delayed oligomer appearance, fibril growth and damage (Fig. 2A). Network-mean dynamics (Fig. 2B) show that the oligomer burden Y grows slowly for the first ~20 time units (9% of its peak at t = 15), accelerates between 20 and 35, and peaks at Ypeak = 0.346 at t = 35.8; the damage variable crosses ⟨a⟩ = 0.2, 0.5 and 0.8 at t = 24.7, 27.8 and 31.5, and saturates at ⟨a⟩end = 0.955 with all nodes exceeding damage 0.8 by the end of the simulation. The phase portrait of the seed region (Fig. 2C) shows the nucleation lag followed by runaway growth: while fibril burden is low, oligomer production is dominated by slow primary nucleation; once fibrils accumulate, secondary nucleation takes over and the trajectory turns sharply upward. Fig. 2D shows the underlying feedback: the production rate λ0(1 − a)(1 + βa) rises with damage up to a* = (β − 1)/2β = 0.39, so that damaged tissue produces more monomer, which feeds more aggregation and more damage — a self-reinforcing loop that terminates only when neuronal loss drives production to zero at a → 1.

### 4.2 Spatial propagation and topology robustness

On the surrogate connectome, pathology spreads outward from the entorhinal seed (Fig. 3A–D): by t = 10 damage is still essentially confined to the entorhinal cortex (a = 0.85), with the parahippocampal cortex beginning to be affected (a = 0.17); by t = 18 the medial temporal lobe is substantially damaged; and by t = 28 damage is widespread, with fusiform, temporal-pole, precuneus and cuneus regions all above 0.55. The region-by-time heat map (Fig. 3E), ordered by onset, reproduces a Braak-like temporal ordering: medial temporal regions first (the entorhinal cortex crosses a = 0.3 at t ≈ 4), followed by parahippocampal, insular and orbitofrontal cortex (t ≈ 13–22), then temporal association areas (t ≈ 22–24) and finally cingulate, parietal and occipital association cortex (t ≈ 24–26) [33]. Spread is governed by network distance from the seed rather than by hubness: the geodesic distance from the entorhinal cortex strongly predicts regional onset time (Pearson r = 0.60, p = 7 × 10−8), whereas node strength predicts neither onset (r = 0.06, p = 0.61) nor terminal damage (r = 0.12, p = 0.32). The absence of a hub effect has two explanations. First, terminal damage saturates: every region reaches aend between 0.95 and 0.96 (s.d. 0.002), leaving almost no inter-regional variance for any static predictor to explain. Second, in a runaway cascade the local aggregation engine, not the wiring diagram, sets each region's fate once pathology arrives. This null result distinguishes the present aggregation-driven model from pure network-diffusion accounts, in which highly connected regions are expected to be preferentially affected [24,25]. Critically, the aggregate trajectory is essentially independent of network topology (Fig. 3F): the cortical sheet, the small-world network and the connectome give Ypeak = 0.346, 0.321 and 0.315 and ⟨a⟩end = 0.955, 0.955 and 0.961, with ⟨a⟩ = 0.5 crossing times of 27.8, 27.2 and 25.1 respectively. The local aggregation kinetics, not the wiring diagram, dominates the outcome — an important robustness result for a model whose connectome is a surrogate.

### 4.3 Bistability and the critical passivation threshold

Continuation of the steady states of the well-mixed node as a function of the passivation fraction (Fig. 4A) reveals a threshold structure that is richer than the linear-stability analysis of Section 2.5 alone suggests, and the distinction between two boundaries is central to the therapeutic interpretation. The **analytic** boundary Eq. (10) is the threshold of the aggregation subsystem with the damage coordinate frozen at a = 0: the fibril-poor state loses stability at θc = 0.547. The **full-system** boundary is obtained by continuing the complete (X, Y, Z, a) node. The two do not coincide. In the full system the diseased state is the unique attractor up to θ ≈ 0.93, where a saddle-node bifurcation creates a pair of new states — a stable low-damage state and an unstable saddle — so that above the fold a healthy attractor (a* ≈ 0.02) coexists with the diseased one (a* ≈ 0.46); the diseased state persists, with declining damage, all the way to θ → 1, where aggregation is switched off entirely. Between θc and the full-system fold lies a wide **feedback gap** of ≈ 0.38 in passivation fraction: the aggregation subsystem is formally stable there, but the damage feedback — production rising with damage up to a* = 0.39, and toxicity-driven damage accumulation — re-ignites the cascade. Forward integration confirms this: at θ = 0.65 a healthy initial condition converges to a = 0.77, and even at θ = 0.90 — within 0.03 of the fold — it still converges to the diseased state (a ≈ 0.54), albeit with pronounced critical slowing down.

The mechanism of the feedback gap is visible in the quasi-steady-state reduction of the slow damage variable (Fig. 4D). With (X, Y, Z) slaved to the damage coordinate, the scalar equation da/dt = f(a) admits a positive equilibrium only where monomer production has fallen far enough, or passivation has suppressed aggregation strongly enough, for the fast subsystem to be contractive. In the untreated system (θ = 0) the slow manifold exists only for a ≳ 0.91: essentially the entire healthy range of the damage coordinate is a runaway region in which no quasi-steady aggregation state exists — the dynamical signature of a cascade that, once initiated, cannot be arrested by repair alone. At θ = 0.65 a short left-hand segment of slow manifold appears at small damage, but it is separated from the diseased well (a* = 0.77) by a runaway gap (a ≈ 0.13–0.65) that a trajectory cannot cross from below; the left segment is therefore transient rather than attracting, which is why the full system still collapses to the diseased state. Only at strong passivation (θ = 0.95) does the slow manifold extend over the whole range and develop the canonical bistable structure: stable fixed points at a* = 0.02 and a* = 0.45, separated by an unstable saddle at a* = 0.09, with a pseudo-potential displaying two wells.

Bistability is confirmed by forward integration of the full node (Fig. 4C): under sustained θ = 0.95, a healthy initial condition converges to a = 0.009 while a diseased initial condition converges to a = 0.45 — two attractors reachable from different initial conditions at identical parameter values. The lower fold (θ ≈ 0.93) is the arrest threshold relevant to prevention and early intervention; escape from the diseased state, by contrast, requires passivation levels at which the diseased well itself becomes shallow, which is why the same dose that prevents disease when applied early fails to reverse it when applied late (Section 4.5).

Fig. 4B maps the therapeutic boundary in the (cY, θ) plane, and the separation of the two boundaries is again the point. The analytic curve θc(cY) falls steeply with oligomer clearance — at cY = 0.5 the subsystem threshold drops from 0.55 to 0.24 — but the numerically located full-system boundary lies systematically above it, by 0.35–0.38 across the range of cY examined (from θ = 0.93 at cY = 0.25 to θ = 0.60 at cY = 0.50). The vertical distance between the two curves is the feedback penalty: the additional passivation that damage feedback makes necessary at each clearance level. Clearance enhancement and passivation act on the same inequality from opposite sides, but their synergy is bounded by this penalty — a conclusion reinforced by the independent long-horizon analysis of Section 4.5. The shaded region above the full-system boundary is the cascade-arrested regime; the intermediate band is the feedback gap, in which the subsystem is stable but the full system is not.

### 4.4 Dose–response of interface passivation

Passivator binding is fast and saturating (Fig. 5A): once the passivator level is established, the occupancy approaches the Langmuir isotherm with KD = 0.15 within a few time units, so that a sustained passivator level P produces an occupancy θ* = P/(0.15 + P). The approach to the sustained level is itself slow, however: with infusion rate uP and decay rate cP, P(t) = (uP/cP)(1 − e^{−cP t}), so that for the reference pharmacokinetics (cP = 0.02) the passivator accumulates with a time constant of 50 time units — comparable to the entire disease transition. This accumulation phase turns out to be therapeutically decisive (Section 4.5). Fig. 5B shows the downstream mechanism: the oligomer wave is suppressed in a dose-dependent manner, with the mean interface activity 1 − θ falling as the passivator accumulates.

Toxicity metrics, which are properties of the early transient, fall monotonically with dose (Fig. 5C): Ypeak decreases from 0.319 at the lowest dose to 0.018 at the highest (a 94% reduction), and AUCY (over the 40-unit observation window) from 4.41 to 0.59 (87%).

Damage metrics require more care, because a finite observation horizon can conflate genuine arrest with critical slowing down (Fig. 5D). Evaluated at t = 40, terminal damage appears to undergo a sharp sigmoidal transition: ⟨a⟩end stays above 0.85 for θ* ≲ 0.65, falls to 0.36 at θ* = 0.79, and approaches 0.03 for θ* ≳ 0.94, with a four-parameter logistic EC50 of θ* ≈ 0.77 (P ≈ 0.50) and a Hill slope of 19. Extended to t = 200, however, the same simulations reveal that intermediate doses merely slow the cascade: the t = 40 value of 0.19 at θ* = 0.83 unravels to 0.64 by t = 200, and even the highest dose (θ* = 0.985) reaches only 0.21. Two mechanisms produce this divergence. First, critical slowing down: below the saddle-node fold (θ_SN ≈ 0.93, Section 4.3) the diseased state is the only attractor, but the approach to it slows dramatically as the fold is neared, so that a 40-unit snapshot catches the trajectory mid-descent. Second, seed commitment during drug accumulation: because θ* is approached with a 50-unit time constant, the effective passivation traverses the sub-threshold range for tens of time units, during which the seed region — which is damaged from the outset in our initial condition — commits to the diseased basin and, by hysteresis, cannot be recovered.

The durable dose–response is therefore graded rather than sharp: a decline from 0.90 to 0.21 as the dose rises from the lowest to the highest value, with its midpoint (θ* ≈ 0.89) lying just below the node-level fold. Durable whole-network protection by monotherapy is thus a kinetic as well as a thermodynamic requirement: the sustained occupancy must exceed the fold (uP ≳ 0.2, θ* ≈ 0.985, some 17-fold the reference dose), and the onset must be early enough that the occupancy crosses the fold before the amplification phase begins. At a t = 0 onset this is achieved — durable damage 0.09 — whereas uP = 0.1, whose crossing comes only at t ≈ 25, is already too late (durable 0.21), and at a t = 8 onset no monotherapy dose durably protects the network even at uP = 1.0 (durable 0.18): by the time the passivator accumulates, the seed region — damaged from the outset — has committed to the diseased basin and local spread has begun. At the reference dose (θ* ≈ 0.80) the plateau never reaches the fold at all, and monotherapy buys time without arresting the cascade whatever the onset. Lowering the fold itself, by clearance enhancement, achieves at the reference dose what monotherapy requires a large overdose to achieve (Section 4.5).

### 4.5 Therapeutic window and combination therapy

The benefit of passivation depends strongly on when it is started, but the answer depends critically on the horizon over which benefit is judged (Fig. 6A, B). Evaluated at t = 40, all three strategies appear effective when started early: passivation from t = 0 holds terminal damage at 0.10, from t = 8 at 0.30, and loses efficacy from t ≈ 12 onward; clearance enhancement holds damage below 0.05 for onsets up to t ≈ 18. Extended to t = 200, however, both single-agent curves flatten: passivation alone converges to a durable damage of 0.66 at every onset time, and clearance enhancement alone to 0.72 — neither strategy arrests the cascade, whatever the onset, and the apparent early protection is the critical-slowing-down transient documented in Section 4.4. The onset dependence that survives the long horizon is therefore not a property of the single agents at all.

The combination of passivation and clearance enhancement behaves qualitatively differently (Fig. 6A, B). Its durable outcome rises only slowly with onset — 0.06 at t = 0, 0.09 at t = 8, 0.11 at t = 16, 0.14 at t = 20 — and stays below 0.16 up to an onset of t ≈ 22, beyond which the seed's commitment to the diseased basin becomes irreversible and the benefit saturates at 0.20–0.23 (the residual damage of the seed and its immediate neighbours). The single-agent flattening and the combination's gradual loss of efficacy are two views of the same threshold structure: the combination lowers the full-system fold (from θ ≈ 0.93 at the reference clearance to ≈ 0.6 at cY = 0.50, and lower still with cZ enhanced), so that the occupancy crossed during the accumulation phase — θ* ≈ 0.80 at the reference dose — lies above the shifted fold, while it lies below the unshifted fold for either agent alone.

Fig. 6C maps the durable design landscape: terminal damage at t = 200 over a grid of clearance rates (cY from 0.25 to 0.60) and passivation doses (uP from 5 × 10−4 to 0.1). The 0.15 contour identifies the durable neuroprotection boundary, which is a ridge running from (cY ≈ 0.3, θ* ≈ 0.97) through (cY = 0.44, θ* ≈ 0.85) to (cY ≥ 0.52, θ* ≈ 0.76): in the low-dose regime the two agents compensate for each other almost exactly, so that a given increment in clearance is equivalent, in damage terms, to a given increment in passivation, and vice versa. The ridge is the network-level image of the full-system saddle-node boundary of Fig. 4B, displaced upward by the seed-commitment effect.

Bliss analysis on the durable outcomes (Fig. 6D) shows that the combination benefit exceeds the Bliss-independent prediction almost everywhere on the grid (mean excess +0.15, with 95% of grid points above the independence line). At the reference operating point the effect is large: the single-agent benefits are 0.22 (passivation) and 0.07 (clearance), the Bliss-predicted combination benefit is 0.27, and the observed benefit is 0.73 — an excess of +0.45. The Bliss framework assumes smooth, saturating dose–response surfaces, and its violation here is precisely the point: in a threshold system the two interventions do not merely add, they enable each other. Passivation alone cannot arrest the cascade at the reference dose, and clearance alone cannot arrest it at any feasible rate, but together they move the system across the fold — the mechanistic content of the supra-additivity, and the quantitative basis for combination regimens in which each agent is used at a dose it could not have been used at alone.

### 4.6 Global sensitivity analysis

PRCC analysis (n = 400 Latin hypercube samples, parameters varied over 3-fold ranges) identifies the parameters that dominate each outcome (Fig. 7A, B; Table 3). The strongest controls on every outcome are the monomer-supply parameters: the production rate λ0 (positive) and the monomer clearance rate cX (negative) have PRCC magnitudes of 0.65–0.85, reflecting that the healthy monomer pool Xs = λ0/cX sets the concentration scale of the entire cascade. Among the aggregation parameters, the secondary-nucleation coefficient ks is the strongest positive driver (PRCC 0.40–0.69 across outcomes), followed by the oligomer clearance cY (−0.35 to −0.61) and the fibril clearance cZ (−0.31 to −0.36); the primary-nucleation rate kn is significant but secondary (0.12–0.36). The damage gain ηY is a major positive driver of terminal damage (+0.46) but a negative driver of Ypeak (−0.43), because faster damage collapses proteostatic production earlier. The ranking is stable across the five outcomes, with one systematic exception: the timing metric t_a_0.5 carries the opposite sign for λ0 and cX (faster supply, earlier transition) and is additionally sensitive to the toxicity threshold KY (+0.44). The network-transmission parameters DY and ηN are not significant for any outcome (|PRCC| < 0.11, p > 0.13), consistent with the topology robustness of Section 4.2. The ensemble forecast (Fig. 7C) shows that parameter uncertainty translates into substantial outcome uncertainty: the 5th–95th percentile band for terminal damage spans 0.00–0.98, while the timing of the ⟨a⟩ = 0.5 crossing ranges from 8 to 40 time units across the ensemble (5th–95th percentile, median 26) — quantifying how much of the inter-individual variability in AD progression could in principle be explained by kinetic differences alone. The dominant-driver scatter (Fig. 7D) confirms a monotone, near-log-linear relation between λ0 and terminal damage (r = 0.50). These results sharpen the therapeutic target hierarchy: the dominant disease drivers (λ0, cX, ηY) are not directly druggable in the present framework, but the actionable parameters — surface reactivity (ks), oligomer clearance (cY), fibril clearance (cZ) and damage gain (ηY) — are precisely those that passivation, nucleation inhibition and clearance enhancement act on, whereas interventions that only slow spatial spread (ηN, DY) affect neither timing nor outcome at the network level.

### 4.7 Parameter identifiability

The synthetic benchmark is recovered accurately (Fig. 8A, B). All six fitted parameters are recovered within 10.9% of their true values, with the linearised standard errors and profile-likelihood 95% intervals (Fig. 8C) excluding the null for every parameter. The tightest constraints are obtained for ks and cY (relative interval widths 20% and 23%), whereas kn, ke and ηY are only weakly constrained (141%, 156% and 156%). The looseness is structured rather than random: ηY is positively correlated with KY (r = 0.56; Fig. 8D), and both control the toxicity-to-damage gain, so the trajectory data constrain their combination more sharply than either parameter separately. Every interval contains the value used to generate the benchmark, indicating that the profile likelihood is well calibrated at this noise level. This near-degeneracy is itself an informative result, because it predicts which parameters cannot be separated from trajectory data alone. The prediction band (Fig. 8A) is narrow, indicating that the fitted trajectory is well determined even though individual parameters are not all independent. Overall, the identifiability analysis establishes that the model's key kinetic parameters are jointly recoverable from three routinely measurable quantities (a toxicity proxy, a fibril-burden proxy and a damage proxy), which is the prerequisite for future calibration against biomarker data.

### 4.8 Comparison of intervention strategies

Table 2 compares five mechanistic intervention strategies applied at t = 8, with terminal damage reported both at the t = 40 observation horizon and at t = 200. The distinction between the two horizons is decisive. At t = 40, clearance enhancement (cY: 0.25 → 0.50, cZ: 0.114 → 0.20) appears highly effective — Ypeak reduced by 92%, terminal damage by 95% — and passivation (uP = 0.012, sustained θ* = 0.80, of which 0.65 is reached by t = 40) appears moderately effective — Ypeak reduced by 77%, damage by 68%. At t = 200 both single agents collapse to a common failure mode: durable damage of 0.725 (clearance) and 0.663 (passivation) against 0.912 untreated, because neither crosses the full-system threshold (Sections 4.3–4.5). Nucleation inhibition (kn: 0.008 → 0.002) is ineffective on both horizons (durable damage 0.911), confirming that secondary rather than primary nucleation dominates the cascade [9,10]. Suppressing network transmission (DY and ηN halved) has no effect on either horizon (0.954 versus 0.955 at t = 40; 0.912 at t = 200): slowing spread without suppressing the local aggregation engine only delays the inevitable. Only the combination of clearance enhancement and passivation durably arrests the cascade — durable damage 0.093, a 90% reduction against baseline, with Ypeak reduced by 96% — the threshold-crossing configuration analysed in Section 4.5.

## 5. Discussion

### 5.1 Principal findings

We have extended the conceptual amyloid-cascade framework into a continuous, network-resolved model in which the fibril interface is an explicit and druggable state. Three findings are, in our view, the most consequential. First, passivation has a sharp, analytically predictable threshold: the linear stability of the oligomer–fibril subsystem gives θc = 1 − (cY + kf)cZ / {Xs[kskf + ke(cY + kf)]}, numerically ≈ 0.55 for the reference parameterisation. Continuation of the full system shows that this subsystem threshold is a lower bound: because damage feeds back on monomer production, the complete node arrests only at θ ≈ 0.93, and the offset — the feedback penalty, 0.35–0.38 across the clearance range examined — is itself a quantitative, mechanistically interpretable result that a purely local analysis would miss. The existence of a threshold is not an artifact of the Hill toxicity function, and it is robust to network topology. Second, the full system is bistable at strong passivation, and the resulting hysteresis defines a therapeutic window: interventions that keep the system below the saddle prevent disease, whereas the same interventions applied after the transition merely slow it — and, crucially, "merely slowing" can masquerade as success over a finite observation horizon. Extended to t = 200, neither single agent arrests the cascade at any onset time (durable damage ≈ 0.66 for passivation, ≈ 0.72 for clearance), so the durable benefit of treatment is governed by whether the intervention crosses the full-system threshold rather than by how early it is started. Third, the dominant parameters are the monomer-supply parameters (λ0, cX), which set the scale of the cascade, and — among the parameters that therapies can act on — the surface-reactivity parameter ks and oligomer clearance cY; passivation and clearance act supra-additively because they enter the same stability inequality from opposite sides, and the synergy takes the specific form of threshold crossing: at the reference operating point each agent alone is sub-threshold, whereas their combination is durably arresting (durable damage 0.093 versus 0.912 untreated), a +0.45 excess over the Bliss-independent prediction.

### 5.2 Relation to previous models

Our framework differs from classical ODE models of Aβ dynamics [18–20] in resolving the aggregation mechanism: instead of an effective aggregation rate, the model contains primary nucleation, surface-catalysed secondary nucleation, elongation and fibril clearance as distinct processes, which is what allows the passivation mechanism and the θc threshold to emerge. Relative to network-diffusion models of AD progression [24,25], we retain explicit local pathology dynamics and show that topology affects the spatial pattern but not the aggregate trajectory: the onset ordering follows geodesic distance from the entorhinal seed (Braak-like staging), while hub status adds no detectable vulnerability once distance is accounted for — a division of labour that suggests network models and kinetic models capture complementary aspects of the disease. Relative to data-driven temporal models [26,27], the present model sacrifices descriptive accuracy for mechanistic interpretability; the identifiability analysis (Section 4.7) is designed to make that trade reversible, by demonstrating that the kinetic parameters can in principle be constrained by trajectory data. Finally, the model inherits from the earlier conceptual framework [30] the smooth toxicity-driven damage and nonlocal damage transmission, but replaces its discrete threshold updates and abstract grid with continuous kinetics and explicit networks.

### 5.3 Biological and clinical implications

The model makes several predictions that are experimentally or clinically addressable. (i) Passivator occupancy, not passivator concentration, is the relevant dose metric: because binding is Langmuir-type, the therapeutic concentration scales with KD, and a high-affinity passivator achieves the same interface coverage at a lower dose. The durable neuroprotection midpoint (θ* ≈ 0.89) corresponds to a passivator level of roughly 8 KD, and full arrest (θ* ≳ 0.93) to ≳ 12 KD — demanding but within the range achievable for small molecules and engineered proteins. (ii) Partial passivation is worthwhile but sub-threshold: doses below the fold slow the cascade and delay onset, which is valuable in a preclinical setting [28,29] but does not change the endpoint — the t = 40 snapshot of an intermediate dose (0.19 at θ* = 0.83) unravels to 0.64 by t = 200, so efficacy claims from short-horizon studies should be read with this critical-slowing caveat. (iii) Combination with clearance enhancement lowers the required passivation fraction substantially (the subsystem threshold falls from 0.55 to ≈ 0.24 when cY is doubled, and the full-system boundary from 0.93 to ≈ 0.60), providing a quantitative rationale for combining interface-targeting agents with immunotherapeutic clearance — consistent with the supra-additivity seen in Fig. 6D, where each agent alone is sub-threshold at the reference dose but the combination is durably arresting. (iv) The model predicts that interventions targeting only the spread of damage (e.g. network-level mechanisms) will fail to alter the endpoint unless they also suppress the local aggregation engine. (v) The therapeutic window is set by the position of the saddle, which in turn depends on the same parameters that PRCC identifies as dominant — suggesting that the parameters measurable from early biomarker trajectories could, in a calibrated version of the model, be used to stratify patients by expected benefit. (vi) Because the passivator accumulates with a 50-unit time constant, the model predicts a kinetic, not merely thermodynamic, constraint on monotherapy: rapid accumulation is as important as the eventual plateau level, which argues for loading-dose designs that cross the threshold before the seed region commits to the diseased basin.

### 5.4 Limitations

Several limitations should be weighed. First, the network is a surrogate: although its macro-scale statistics are realistic and the results are topology-robust, patient-specific connectomes would be required for individual-level prediction. Second, the model resolves only the amyloid cascade; tau pathology, neuroinflammation, vascular contributions and synaptic dysfunction are absent, and the damage variable is a lumped index of neuronal health. Third, time and concentration are dimensionless; mapping to physical units requires external calibration, and the parameter values in Table 1 are reference working points chosen to reproduce the qualitative phenomenology of sporadic AD, not fitted physiological values. Fourth, the passivation mechanism is idealised as a single occupancy state with linear suppression of both surface reactions; real passivators may bind with negative or positive cooperativity, may distinguish fibril ends from sides, and may remodel fibrils rather than merely cap them [16,17]. Fifth, the identifiability analysis uses a synthetic benchmark; real biomarker data carry systematic as well as statistical errors, and the near-degeneracy between ηY and KY (Fig. 8D) would need to be resolved with independent measurements. Sixth, the analysis of bistability is performed on the well-mixed node; the network system may exhibit additional spatiotemporal patterns (e.g. travelling fronts or re-entrant dynamics) that the node-level reduction cannot capture, although the topology-robustness of the aggregate trajectory suggests these do not alter the main conclusions.

### 5.5 Future directions

Natural extensions include: coupling the amyloid module to a tau propagation module and to an inflammatory response, which would allow the model to address the ordering of pathologies and the limited clinical efficacy of amyloid-only interventions [2,11]; calibration against longitudinal biomarker cohorts [27,34] using the identifiability machinery developed here; replacement of the surrogate connectome with individual structural connectomes for patient-specific simulation; and enrichment of the passivation description with explicit end-versus-side binding, cooperativity and fibril remodelling, which the present framework can accommodate by replacing the scalar occupancy θ with a small binding-state system.

## 6. Conclusions

We presented a continuous-time, network-resolved extension of the amyloid-cascade model in which the fibril interface is an explicit, passivatable state. The model unifies aggregation kinetics, spatial propagation and intervention analysis, and yields four quantitatively useful results. (i) An analytic critical passivation fraction (θc ≈ 0.55) at which the aggregation subsystem is stable — a lower bound that damage feedback raises to θ ≈ 0.93 in the full system, the difference being a mechanistically interpretable feedback penalty of 0.35–0.38. (ii) A bistable regime at strong passivation with a therapeutic window that quantifies the cost of delayed treatment. (iii) A horizon-dependence caution with practical force: below the saddle-node fold, intermediate doses produce critical slowing that mimics protection over a 40-unit observation window while the cascade continues to completion by t = 200, so single-agent efficacy is governed by threshold crossing rather than onset time — and monotherapy crosses the threshold durably only at ≳ 17-fold the reference dose. (iv) A supra-additive interaction between interface passivation and clearance enhancement that maps a durable-arrest ridge across the combination dose space: at the reference operating point each agent alone is sub-threshold, while the combination durably arrests the cascade (durable damage 0.093 versus 0.912 untreated), a +0.45 excess over the Bliss-independent prediction. Global sensitivity analysis identifies monomer supply as the dominant control on disease outcome, with secondary nucleation and oligomer clearance as the leading actionable control points, and identifiability analysis confirms that the governing parameters are recoverable from trajectory data. The framework provides a mechanistically explicit, mathematically transparent basis for evaluating fibril-interface-targeting therapies and for designing combination regimens against Alzheimer's disease.
